On the plane and after you land

What you do on the flight and in the first day or two decides how quickly you settle.

During the flight

The CDC and the NHS give very similar advice for the flight itself:

  • Set your watch to local time at your destination as soon as you board, and time your meals and sleep by it. If it is night at your destination, try to sleep; if it is day there, try to stay awake.
  • Drink water. Being short of water can make the physical symptoms of jet lag worse, according to the CDC.
  • Go easy on alcohol and caffeine. The NHS says too much of either can make jet lag worse. The CDC adds that alcohol may help you fall asleep but breaks up the sleep, which is why it does not give good rest.
  • Keep moving. Stretch and walk around the cabin now and then, as the NHS advises.
  • Help yourself sleep. An eye mask and earplugs can help if you want to sleep.

The first day after you land

The key idea is to live on local time from the moment you arrive.

  1. Get outdoors in daylight. The NHS and the CDC both stress natural light. Light is the main signal that moves your body clock. For the direction of your trip, see why east is harder than west: after flying east, morning light is the most useful; after flying west, evening light.
  2. Stay awake through the local day. Staying awake builds up the need for sleep, which helps you sleep at local night, the CDC explains.
  3. Nap only briefly if you must. A nap of 20 to 30 minutes can keep you alert, but long naps in the day make it harder to sleep at night, and the NHS advises sleeping only at night.
  4. Eat at local times. Meals are a clock signal too.
  5. Go to bed at the local bedtime, not much earlier, even if you feel you could sleep now.
  6. Set an alarm for the first morning so that you do not oversleep, as the NHS suggests.

If you wake in the night

It is common to wake at 3 or 4 in the morning in the first nights. Do not check the time over and over. Stay in a dark room, rest quietly, and try not to look at bright screens. If you cannot sleep after a while, sit in dim light for a short time and then go back to bed. Do not make up for the lost sleep with a long sleep-in, because that delays the adjustment.

Be kind to the first days

While your body clock catches up, your concentration and memory can be poor. Avoid driving if you are very sleepy. Do not take big decisions. Plan easy activities, and leave the demanding ones for later in the trip. Keep up your water, and eat light meals if your stomach is upset.

When the symptoms do not ease

For most people, symptoms ease over a few days. If you still have trouble sleeping, are very tired during the day for a long time, or have other symptoms that worry you, speak to a doctor. Medicines are not usually needed for jet lag, according to the NHS, and the decision on any medicine belongs with a doctor.

Quick plan for the first 48 hours

  • Land, set your watch, eat at a local mealtime.
  • Walk outdoors in daylight.
  • Keep any nap short, and before mid-afternoon.
  • Go to bed at the local bedtime.
  • Next day: repeat, and add a longer outdoor walk.
Should I sleep on the flight?

If it is night at your destination, yes, try. If it is daytime there, try to stay awake so that you are tired at the local bedtime.

What if I arrive in the middle of the night?

Go to bed and sleep, avoid bright light, and then get daylight after you wake.

Wellness information, not medical advice. The plans here follow general guidance from the CDC Yellow Book (2026 edition, page dated 23 April 2025) and the NHS (page last reviewed 31 May 2023). If you have a medical condition, take regular medicines, or are considering melatonin or sleeping tablets, talk to a doctor before you travel.
