Before you fly: preparing for the time change

A little preparation before you leave makes the first days at the other end easier.

Start with sleep

Both official sources agree that the best thing you can do before a long trip is to arrive rested. The NHS advises getting plenty of rest before you travel. If you can, the CDC suggests shifting your sleep towards the destination time for a few days before the trip: about one hour a day for two to three days. For an eastward trip, that means going to bed and getting up an hour earlier each day. For a westward trip, an hour later each day. Even a shift of an hour or two helps, and you should never do it by cutting your sleep short.

The planner on this site writes out a bedtime schedule for your own trip, based on the time you normally go to bed.

Choose your flight with light in mind

The CDC suggests choosing a flight that arrives at a time that gives you the best light exposure at the destination. In practice, arriving in the morning or afternoon usually lets you spend daylight hours outdoors and then go to sleep at the local bedtime. A very late arrival can leave you with little daylight to use, and a very early arrival can mean a long wait before you can go to bed. There is no perfect answer; think about what your first day needs.

Plan the first days

  • Keep the first day light. Do not schedule an important meeting for the first morning if you can help it.
  • Know your local bedtime, and plan to stay up until then, even if you are very tired.
  • Check where you will get daylight: a hotel with a balcony, a park nearby, a place for a walk.
  • Book a room that you can sleep in at the hours you will need, with curtains or an eye mask for early light.

Pack for sleep and comfort

An eye mask and earplugs help many people sleep on the flight and in a strange room, as the NHS notes. Bring a water bottle that you can fill after security, a warm layer, and headphones. Put anything you will need at night within easy reach.

Look after yourself in the days before

  • Do not start the trip tired. Finish packing the day before, and avoid a late night before an early flight.
  • Eat normally and keep up your water.
  • Be careful with alcohol and caffeine in the days around the trip, because both can interfere with sleep.

What about medicines?

This site does not suggest medicines. The CDC's chapter, written for health professionals, describes timed melatonin and other medicines as options that need careful timing, because taking melatonin at the wrong time can make things worse. The NHS says it does not recommend melatonin tablets for jet lag because the evidence is not strong enough, and that sleeping tablets can be addictive and should only be used for a short time when symptoms are severe. If you want to consider either, talk to a doctor before you travel, particularly if you have a health condition or take other medicines.

A simple checklist

  1. Two or three days before: shift bedtime by about an hour a day toward the destination.
  2. The day before: pack, rest, and drink water.
  3. On the day: set your watch to destination time as you board.
  4. Pack an eye mask, earplugs and a water bottle.
  5. Plan your first day: daylight, local bedtime, no big decisions.
Is it worth shifting sleep for a short trip?

For a trip of two or three days the NHS suggests you could keep your home schedule instead. See short trips and frequent travel.

What if I cannot shift my sleep before I leave?

Then skip that step and focus on the flight and the first days. It helps, but it is not required.

Wellness information, not medical advice. The plans here follow general guidance from the CDC Yellow Book (2026 edition, page dated 23 April 2025) and the NHS (page last reviewed 31 May 2023). If you have a medical condition, take regular medicines, or are considering melatonin or sleeping tablets, talk to a doctor before you travel.
