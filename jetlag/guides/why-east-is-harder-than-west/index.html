Why flying east is harder than flying west

Most people find eastward trips harder. There is a reason, and it shapes how to plan.

Your clock runs a little long

Your internal body clock does not run on exactly 24 hours. For most people it is slightly longer. The CDC Yellow Book gives this as the reason: people naturally want to go to bed a bit later each night, even without travel. That makes it easier to push your clock later than to pull it earlier.

Advancing and delaying

Sleep researchers use two words. Advancing the body clock means moving it earlier, so that you want to fall asleep and wake up earlier. Delaying means moving it later, so that you want to go to bed and get up later.

When you fly east, the clock on the wall at your destination is ahead of your body clock: it shows a later time than your body expects. To fit in, you have to advance your body clock, which is the harder direction. When you fly west, the clock on the wall is behind your body clock, and you have to delay your body clock, which suits its natural tendency.

The numbers

The CDC gives average rates of adaptation: about 1.5 hours a day for westward travel and about 1 hour a day for eastward travel. These are averages across many people. They are the numbers this site uses for its planning estimates. For a gap of six hours, that is about four days westward and six days eastward.

Big gaps

When the time difference is very large, around nine hours or more, the body can adjust in either direction. A twelve-hour gap is the same distance whichever way you go round. Our planner shows both estimates for large gaps and suggests planning for the longer one, to be safe.

What this means for your plan

  • Going east: start shifting earlier before you fly. Go to bed and get up earlier for a couple of days. After you land, bright morning light helps your clock move earlier, and bright light late in the evening works against you.
  • Going west: shift later before you fly if you can. After you land, bright evening light helps your clock move later, and very early bright light works against you.
  • Either way: do not cut your sleep short while you shift, and stay awake until local bedtime after you land.

Timing of light

The CDC explains the timing in more detail. Bright light in the morning after your body clock's lowest point, which falls about two to four hours before you normally wake, pushes the clock earlier. Bright light in the evening pushes it later. The best times depend on the direction and on how many time zones you cross, so for the exact timing the CDC suggests a jet lag calculator. Our planner gives the simple version of the rule and does not claim to give exact clock times of light.

Why it matters for planning a trip

If you can choose, a westward trip leaves you with less to fight. If you cannot, plan the first days of an eastward trip so that nothing important falls on the first morning, when your body thinks it is the middle of the night. Give yourself a day to settle if you can, and keep important meetings for when you are at your best.

Is it always worse flying east?

It is on average, but individuals vary, and the number of time zones matters more than direction for small gaps.

Does flying north or south cause jet lag?

No, not by itself. Jet lag comes from changing time zones, which means moving east or west.

Wellness information, not medical advice. The plans here follow general guidance from the CDC Yellow Book (2026 edition, page dated 23 April 2025) and the NHS (page last reviewed 31 May 2023). If you have a medical condition, take regular medicines, or are considering melatonin or sleeping tablets, talk to a doctor before you travel.
