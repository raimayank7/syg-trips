Short trips and frequent travel

Not every trip is worth adapting to. For short stays, the best plan may be to stay on home time.

Very short trips

The NHS advises that if your trip is short, two or three days, you could try not changing your eating and sleeping times to the new time zone. The reason is that adapting takes about as long as the trip itself, and you would then have to adapt back when you return. The CDC's chapter lists the same idea among the strategies for frequent travellers: maintaining home-base sleep hours during short trips where adaptation would be limited.

This only works if your commitments allow it. If your meetings, sightseeing or family events happen on local time, you cannot sleep through them, and you will need to adjust somewhat. A halfway approach is to keep your sleep as close to home time as the schedule allows, and to use light and short naps to stay alert when you need to.

Who this suits

Staying on home time suits people who go for a day or two to a place with a large time difference, for example for a meeting or a short event, and who can keep their hours. It suits people who fly out and back quickly and can plan their activities for the hours when they would be awake at home. It does not suit people whose trips are tied to local business hours, or those staying longer than three days.

Longer trips

For trips of a week or more, adapting is worth it. Follow the steps in the other guides: shift sleep before, use light, eat and sleep on local time, and expect a few days of catching up. See before you fly and on the plane and after you land.

Frequent travellers

The CDC notes that planning is most important for frequent travellers, because poor sleep and a disturbed body clock, repeated again and again, add up to a significant health cost. It says that the effects of repeated jet lag are cumulative, so a frequent flyer cannot simply "get used to it". Ways to cope that the chapter mentions include planning flights to give the best light at the destination, shifting sleep in advance, strategic scheduling of sleep, and staying on home hours for short trips.

Practical habits for people who fly often:

  • Protect sleep before every trip. Do not start tired.
  • Choose flights and arrival times with the first day in mind.
  • Keep a regular routine at home between trips, so the body clock does not drift.
  • Build in a rest day after long trips, if you can.
  • Watch alcohol and caffeine around flights.

Health and medicines

The CDC chapter is written for health professionals and lists medicines used under medical advice for frequent travellers. This site does not recommend any. If you travel often and are considering any medicine or supplement, discuss it with a doctor who can look at your whole health picture.

Choosing: a rule of thumb

  • Trip of up to three days and you can keep your hours: consider staying on home time.
  • Trip of more than three days: adapt to local time.
  • Large time difference and a fixed schedule on local time: shift sleep before you go, and plan the first day to be gentle.
Does staying on home time mean I will be tired at the meeting?

You may be, if the meeting falls in your night. Use daylight, a short nap and water, and avoid scheduling important sessions for your lowest hours.

Is it harder to come home or to go away?

It depends on direction: east is harder on average, for the outward or the return journey.

Wellness information, not medical advice. The plans here follow general guidance from the CDC Yellow Book (2026 edition, page dated 23 April 2025) and the NHS (page last reviewed 31 May 2023). If you have a medical condition, take regular medicines, or are considering melatonin or sleeping tablets, talk to a doctor before you travel.
