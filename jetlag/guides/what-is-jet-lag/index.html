What jet lag is and what it feels like

Jet lag is your body clock being on the wrong time. It is temporary, common and manageable.

The short version

Jet lag is the sleep trouble and tiredness that follow a quick flight across time zones. It happens because your body has an internal clock that keeps its own rhythm of sleep, alertness, hunger and body temperature, and that rhythm takes time to move to a new local time. In the meantime, you feel sleepy when it is bright morning where you are, and wide awake in the middle of the night. The NHS describes it as your normal sleep pattern being disturbed after a long flight, which usually improves within a few days as your body adjusts.

What it feels like

The CDC Yellow Book describes jet lag disorder as more than a general feeling of low energy. The commonest signs are trouble falling asleep at bedtime and trouble waking in the morning, tiredness and exhaustion, being unable to stay awake during the day, poor sleep, and trouble concentrating and remembering. Some people also get an upset stomach, nausea, constipation, a changed appetite, or mild anxiety. The diagnostic criteria used by sleep medicine involve insomnia or excessive daytime sleepiness after crossing at least two time zones, with the daytime effects beginning within a day or two of travel.

What affects how bad it is

Several things make it better or worse:

  • How many time zones you cross. A bigger gap means more to adjust.
  • Direction. Travelling east is usually harder than travelling west: see why east is harder.
  • Light. Getting daylight at the right time helps your clock move; being indoors at the wrong time slows it.
  • Sleep lost on the way. Arriving already short of sleep makes everything worse.
  • You. People differ: the CDC lists individual genetic differences, the use of medicines and other personal factors among the risk factors, so one person can hardly notice it while another is knocked out for days.

How long it lasts

There is no single answer. The CDC gives a planning figure: on average the body clock adjusts by about 1.5 hours a day when travelling west and about 1 hour a day when travelling east. A gap of six hours eastward therefore takes about six days at the average rate, and the same gap westward about four. These are averages from research and your own pace may be faster or slower.

What helps

The advice from both sources is similar. Get good sleep before you go. Stay hydrated and go easy on alcohol and caffeine on the flight. On arrival, switch to local time as quickly as you can, go out in daylight, and sleep at night rather than in the day. If you need a nap, keep it short. The other guides on this site go through each step: before you fly, on the plane and after you land and short trips and frequent travel.

When to see a doctor

Jet lag usually goes away by itself. If you have ongoing sleep problems after travel, if you have a medical condition or take regular medicines, or if you are thinking about melatonin or sleeping tablets, talk to a doctor. The NHS notes that sleeping tablets can be addictive and should only be used briefly when symptoms are severe, and that it does not recommend melatonin tablets for jet lag because the evidence is not strong enough. The CDC's chapter, written for health professionals, discusses timed melatonin as an option, which is why the decision belongs with a doctor.

Is jet lag the same as being tired after a long flight?

Not quite. Tiredness fades after a night's sleep. Jet lag lasts for days because the body clock itself is out of step with local time.

Can you get jet lag from a short time difference?

A gap of one or two hours is usually mild. The diagnostic criteria in the CDC's chapter start from two time zones.
