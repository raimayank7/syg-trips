Travelling with elderly parents: a planning guide

With elders, the best trips are slower, shorter and simpler than you would plan for yourself. Comfort and a calm pace matter more than the number of places seen.

Choose the trip around them

  • Ask what they want. Many elders prefer one place they have long wished to see, a temple town or a relative's city, over a tour of five.
  • Fewer changes of place. One base for the whole trip, with day outings, beats a new hotel every night.
  • Shorter travel days. Break long journeys with a night's halt.
  • Mind the climate and height. Extreme heat, cold and high altitude are hard on older bodies. Ask their doctor before a hill or high-altitude trip, particularly with heart, lung or blood pressure conditions.
  • Avoid peak crowds. Go on weekdays and outside festival rush where the purpose allows.

See the doctor first

Two to three weeks before a long trip:

  1. A check-up, and the doctor's advice on whether the journey suits them.
  2. A written list of conditions, medicines with doses, and allergies, with the doctor's phone number.
  3. Enough medicine for the trip plus a week, split between two bags.
  4. Advice on meal and medicine timing across long travel days and time zones.
  5. For trips abroad, travel insurance that covers their age and existing conditions; read what it excludes.

Carry the medical summary on paper and as a photo on two phones.

Booking travel

  • Trains: ask for lower berths when booking, and choose a coach class with easier access and cleaner washrooms where the budget allows. Railways have provisions for senior citizens; check the current rules on the railway's official website when you book. See our train journey list.
  • Flights: request wheelchair assistance when booking or at least two days ahead; airlines provide it from the kerb to the aircraft door and it eases long walks and queues even for those who can walk. Choose aisle seats near the front and, where possible, direct flights.
  • Road: a comfortable vehicle with easy entry, stops every hour and a half to two hours, and no night driving.
  • Reach stations and airports early. Hurry is the main cause of falls.
  • Avoid very early departures and late-night arrivals.

Choosing where to stay

  • A ground-floor room or a working lift. Ask directly; "first floor" in the hills can mean forty steps.
  • A western toilet, grab bars if possible, a non-slip bathroom floor, hot water.
  • Close to the main place of the visit, to cut daily travel.
  • Food available on the premises or next door.
  • A doctor or hospital within reach: ask the hotel.
  • Rooms next to or connecting with yours.

Call the hotel to confirm these. Photographs online do not show stairs.

The pace of each day

  • One outing a day, in the morning when energy is best, and rest after lunch.
  • Build in sitting time everywhere. Carry a light folding stool for queues.
  • Use autos, taxis and battery vehicles even for short distances; save their strength for the place itself.
  • Check for steps, slopes and distance at each site before going. Many shrines and monuments offer wheelchairs, palanquins or battery cars for elders; ask at the official counter.
  • Do not skip meals or stretch gaps between them.

Food and water

Familiar, freshly cooked, mild food. Bottled or boiled water only. Carry what they are used to: their biscuits, tea, a little home food for the first day, and anything needed for diabetic or low-salt diets. Keep glucose or sweets handy for those on diabetes medicines.

What to carry for them

  • Medicines in a pill organiser marked by day, plus the spare supply.
  • Medical summary, health insurance card, identity proof and its copies.
  • Spectacles and a spare pair; hearing aid batteries; dentures kit.
  • Walking stick, with a rubber tip in good condition.
  • Comfortable, closed footwear with grip; no new shoes.
  • A warm layer and cap, even in summer, for air-conditioned travel.
  • A small pillow or neck support, and a light shawl.
  • A phone they can use, charged, with your numbers on speed dial, and a card in the pocket with name, your numbers and the hotel.
  • Their usual items for sleep and digestion.

The packing list maker gives the base list to add these to.

Safety and dignity

  • Agree a meeting point everywhere, and keep one younger person beside them in crowds.
  • Bathrooms are where falls happen: a night light, a dry floor, and footwear with grip.
  • Let them set the pace and make choices. Being hurried or treated as luggage spoils a trip more than any discomfort.

If something goes wrong

Know the nearest hospital at each stop. For chest pain, breathlessness, sudden weakness, confusion or a fall with pain, do not wait for it to pass: get medical help at once. Keep the insurer's emergency number handy.

What is a good trip length with elderly parents?

Four to seven days with one or two bases suits most. Shorter is better than rushed.

Should elders travel to high-altitude places?

Only with their doctor's advice, and with a gradual climb and rest days. Some conditions make it unsafe.
