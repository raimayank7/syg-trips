How to visit monuments respectfully

Old stone is more fragile than it looks. A few habits keep monuments standing for the next visitor.

Leave it as you found it

The first rule is the oldest: take nothing and leave nothing. Do not pick up stones, tiles or pieces of carving as souvenirs, even if they are lying loose, because loose pieces often mark where a structure is slowly falling apart and archaeologists may need them in place. Do not write, scratch or carve names on walls, however many others have done so; graffiti on old plaster and stone cannot be removed without damage. Take your rubbish with you, including food wrappers and bottles.

Do not climb or lean

Walls, domes, platforms and carved figures were built to stand in their place, not to carry visitors. Climbing on a ruin can bring down stones and can injure you. Do not sit on carvings, hang from railings, or lean a bike against a wall. If there is a barrier or a rope, stay behind it. Many ancient steps and stairways are worn smooth and uneven, so go slowly and wear shoes with a good grip.

Be careful with touching

The oils from many hands wear away carved surfaces and old paintings over time. Where there is a sign asking you not to touch, follow it. Where there is no sign, touch lightly and rarely, and not at all if the surface is painted or looks crumbling. Never rub paper over carvings or inscriptions to copy them, and do not pour water or use chemicals to clean stone.

Photography and drones

Rules differ from site to site: some allow photography freely, some ban flash, tripods or video, and some charge for them. Flash can damage fragile paintings. Drones are controlled by law in India and are often banned near monuments; do not fly one without checking the rules and having permission. Be considerate when you photograph people, and ask before you photograph worshippers or families.

Respect places of worship

Many protected monuments are still in use as temples, mosques, churches, shrines or dargahs. In those, local custom comes first: cover your head or shoulders where expected, remove your shoes where that is the rule, dress modestly, be quiet, and follow the lead of worshippers. Do not enter a part that is closed to visitors. Some places have different rules for different groups of visitors, so ask a custodian if you are unsure.

Think about the people and the land

Many small monuments stand on farmland or beside villages. Keep to paths, close gates behind you, do not walk through crops, and treat people with courtesy. If a local person offers to guide you, you may decide whether to accept, and it is polite to agree a fair payment first if you do. Do not feed or tease animals, and watch out for snakes and insects around old ruins, especially in the rains.

Look, learn, and report problems

Read the signboard, because it is the ASI's notice for the site. Ask the custodian or a local person what the place means to them. If you see damage, dumping or unsafe construction near a monument, report it to the local ASI office or the district authorities, with the monument's name and a photograph. Our guide to what protection means explains why that matters.

Is it an offence to damage a protected monument?

Yes: the law punishes damage to a protected monument. We do not state penalties here. The simple advice is to leave the monument as you found it.

Can I take photographs?

Often yes, but rules vary by site and by kind of camera use. Look for signs and ask the custodian.

What should I carry when visiting remote monuments?

Water, sun protection, good shoes, a charged phone, and the contact of someone who knows your plan. Check the access in advance.
