What "centrally protected monument" means

The phrase appears on blue signboards across India. Here is what lies behind it, in plain words.

Two kinds of protected monuments

In India, an old building or site can be protected at two levels. Some are protected by the central government, which looks after them through the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), a government body under the Ministry of Culture. Others are protected by a state government, through the state's own archaeology department. The list on this site is the first kind only: the monuments and sites that have been declared "of national importance". A great many fine old buildings are protected only by a state, or not protected at all, so a place missing from this list is not necessarily unprotected or unimportant.

How a monument becomes protected

The usual route is a notification under the central law on ancient monuments and archaeological sites and remains, as we understand it a law of 1958 as amended. The government publishes its intention to declare a monument of national importance, allows objections for a period, and then declares it. After that the ASI is responsible for conserving it, and the law places limits on what anyone may do on or near it. Monuments that were already protected under earlier laws were carried across. Because this is a legal process, the list changes over time: monuments are added, and a few are removed. That is why every page here carries the date on which we took the list.

What the law does in outline

In outline, protection means that the monument may not be damaged, altered, defaced or moved without permission, and that building and mining near it are controlled. As we understand it, there is a zone close around the monument where new construction is prohibited and a wider zone beyond it where construction is regulated and needs permission. Offences are punishable. We do not state distances or penalties here, because they have been amended and you should read the current law or ask the ASI if it matters to you, for example if you own land near a monument.

What it means for a visitor

For most visitors the practical meaning is simple. A protected monument is looked after by the ASI, which may charge an entry fee and set opening hours at the ones that receive visitors, may ban certain things such as climbing, touching, flash photography or drones, and may close parts for repairs. Many entries on the list are not "attractions" at all: a single gateway beside a road, a mound in a field, a stretch of old wall, a stone inscription. They may have no staff, no ticket and no fixed hours, and some stand on private or farm land with only a path to reach them.

The signboard

At many protected sites you will see a signboard that names the monument, says that it is protected under the law, and warns against damaging it. Read the board, since it is the ASI's notice for that site and may include the specific rules. If something looks wrong, for example a monument being damaged, you can inform the local ASI office; the circle names in the list show which office looks after each state.

What the list does not tell you

The list names each monument with a locality and a district. It does not give a date, a style, a description, a ticket price or a timing. For those, use the ASI's own pages for the better-known sites, the state tourism office, and local people. Our guide to reading the list explains how to make the most of what it does contain.

Is a monument that is not in this list unprotected?

Not necessarily. It may be protected by a state government, or be a living place of worship or private property that is looked after in other ways. This list covers only the centrally protected ones.

Does "protected" mean open to the public?

No. Some protected monuments are open with a ticket, some are open freely, and many are ruins, mounds or small structures with no organised access. Check before you go.

Who do I ask about a specific monument?

The Archaeological Survey of India's circle office for the state, or the state tourism office. The list shows which circle covers each state.
