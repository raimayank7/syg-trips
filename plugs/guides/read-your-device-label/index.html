How to read the power label on your devices

The small print on a charger or appliance tells you everything you need to know about travelling with it.

Where to look

On a phone, tablet or laptop charger, the label is printed on the plastic of the power brick, in very small letters. On an appliance such as a hair dryer or iron, look at the base or on a metal plate near the cord. If you cannot read it, hold it under a bright light or take a photo and zoom in.

Input and output

A charger has an input side, which is the mains, and an output side, which is what goes into your device. Only the input matters for travel.

A typical input line reads: "INPUT: 100-240V ~ 50/60Hz 0.5A". The three parts mean:

  • 100-240V: the range of mains voltage the charger accepts. The wavy line ("~") means alternating current, which is what a wall socket supplies.
  • 50/60Hz: the mains frequency it accepts. Almost all chargers accept both.
  • 0.5A: the current it draws, in amperes.

The output line, for example "OUTPUT: 5V 2A", says what the charger gives to your phone. It has nothing to do with the country.

Volts, hertz, amps and watts

Volts measure the electrical pressure. India's household supply is 230 volts; North America's is 120. Hertz is how many times per second the alternating current changes direction: 50 in India, 60 in North America. Amps measure how much current flows. Watts measure power, and equal volts times amps. A 1,000-watt hair dryer on 230 volts draws a bit over four amps.

Reading the result

If the input range includes both your home voltage and the destination's, the device works there without a converter. "100-240V" covers India (230 V), North America (120 V), Japan (100 V) and almost everywhere else. A label with only "220-240V" or "230V" covers India and Europe but not North America.

Frequency matters less. Chargers do not care. Devices that use a motor tied to the mains frequency, or a clock that counts mains cycles, run a bit fast or slow on the other frequency.

Common cases

  • Phone, tablet and laptop chargers: nearly always 100-240 V. You need a plug adapter only.
  • Cameras, power banks and electric toothbrush chargers: usually 100-240 V; check.
  • Hair dryers, straighteners, irons and kettles: often single-voltage, and high power. Check carefully, or buy one at your destination.
  • Electric shavers: many are 100-240 V; check.
  • Devices with a motor, such as a blender: usually single-voltage; do not take them abroad.

When there is no label

If you cannot find a label, treat the device as single-voltage and do not plug it into a different supply. The plug shape tells you nothing about the voltage.

What does the CE or BIS mark on a label mean?

It is a safety or conformity mark, not a voltage rating. Read the numbers next to "Input".

Is a USB power bank affected by the country's voltage?

The power bank is not, but the charger you use to refill it is. Refill it with a charger marked 100-240 V.

Check before you travel. Plug, voltage and frequency data come from the IEC World Plugs list as archived on 2 June 2024. Standards and wiring change, and hotels often have extra sockets or none. Check your device label and ask your hotel or airline for anything that matters, such as medical equipment.
