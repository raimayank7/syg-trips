How the height of a mountain is measured

A mountain's height sounds like a single fact. In practice it is a measurement with choices built in.

Height above what?

The height of a mountain is quoted above "sea level", but the sea is not level. It is pulled up and down by gravity, which varies from place to place, and by tides and currents. To make heights comparable, countries and scientists use a model of the average sea surface continued under the land, called a geoid, and measure from that. Different countries have used different models over the years, tied to a tide gauge at a particular port, so a height measured in one country can differ by a metre or more from a height measured with another country's reference. This is one reason why the same peak can have two numbers.

How heights are measured

Old surveys used levelling and triangulation: measuring angles and distances from known points and working out heights by geometry. Many of the numbers still in use come from surveys of that kind. Modern surveys use satellite navigation receivers placed on the summit, which record signals for hours to fix the position precisely, and ground-based radar or laser instruments. Satellites also measure heights from space by radar and by laser, producing maps of whole mountain ranges. Each method has an error of a metre or less on a good day, and more on a bad one.

Snow, rock and the "true" summit

A summit may be covered by snow or ice, and the thickness of the snow changes from year to year. Is the height that of the rock beneath or of the snow on top? Different countries and surveys have answered differently, and this has been argued over the highest mountain in the world. Wikidata, the source of the figures on this site, records several values for it, within a few metres of each other. When a mountain has several values we show the middle one and say that sources differ.

Why mountains move

Mountains are not fixed. Plates of the Earth's crust push against each other, and some ranges rise by millimetres each year while erosion wears them down. Earthquakes can raise or lower a summit by a visible amount. These changes are small compared with measurement error in most cases, but they mean that a height measured long ago may differ from a measurement made today.

Height is not the same as prominence

Two other numbers are often confused with height. Prominence measures how much a peak stands out: the height you must climb from the lowest pass that connects it to a higher mountain. A mountain with a small prominence is a subsidiary summit of a bigger one. Isolation is the distance to the nearest higher point. A mountain can be very high yet have a modest prominence if it sits on a high ridge.

Where Wikidata gives a prominence we show it on the page. Climbers and mountain lists often use prominence to decide what counts as a separate mountain.

How to use the numbers

Use the heights on this site to compare, not to climb. A difference of a few metres between two peaks is within the uncertainty of the measurements, so do not read much into it. The ranking of the highest mountains is clear at the top, where the gaps are large; further down, many peaks are close in height and their order may change with a new survey.

Is Everest still the highest mountain?

Above sea level, yes, by a clear margin among mountains on land. Other measures, such as distance from the Earth's centre or height from the base on the ocean floor, give different answers for other mountains.

Why do two websites give different heights for the same peak?

They may be quoting different surveys, different reference levels or different dates, or one may include snow and the other not.

About these figures. Heights, areas and lengths come from Wikidata (CC0), downloaded on 14 November 2026. Measurements differ between sources, and rivers and lakes change; where Wikidata holds several values we use the middle one and say so. Treat the numbers as good estimates, not survey results.
