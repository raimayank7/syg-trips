How to read the rankings: units, ranks and ties

A few notes make the rankings easier to use and harder to misread.

Units

Heights are given in metres and in feet, areas in square kilometres and in square miles, and lengths in kilometres and in miles. The metric figure comes first because it is the one in the data. The imperial figures are worked out from it by fixed factors: one metre is about 3.28 feet, one square kilometre is about 0.386 square miles, and one kilometre is about 0.621 miles. We round to whole numbers, so the conversions can differ by one unit from a source that converts a more precise figure.

What a rank means

A rank is the position in our list, not in the world. Our list of mountains contains the mountains that Wikidata links to many Wikipedia editions and that have a recorded height, a country on Earth and a recorded value. That is a large list, but it is not every mountain. A mountain may be missing because it is little known, because its data are poor, or because its values disagree.

The same is true of the lakes and the rivers. If you see a rank of 300, read it as "the 300th largest of the lakes we list", and expect that a complete survey might put it somewhere else.

Country ranks

On each page we also give a rank within the first country listed. This is a rank within our list for that country, so a country with few entries gives a small rank. Features that cross borders appear under each country they touch.

Ties and near-ties

Where two features have the same figure, the order is alphabetical, not a judgement. Where two figures differ by less than the uncertainty of the measurement, their order is a coin toss. A few metres in the height of a peak is within the error of most surveys, a few square kilometres is often within the seasonal change of a lake, and tens of kilometres in the length of a river are within the dispute over its source. Use the "close in the ranking" lists on each page to see how crowded the neighbourhood is.

What the rankings leave out

  • Features on other bodies. Only features on Earth are included.
  • Extinct and composite lakes. Ice-age lakes that have dried up, and combinations of two lakes, are excluded.
  • Small and little-known features. To be listed a feature needs entries in many language editions of Wikipedia, which favours well-known places.
  • Partial measurements. Where a feature has several recorded values, any below 75% of the largest are ignored, because they usually measure a part of it.
  • Undersea features. Submarine mountains are not listed, so the height of a mountain here is always its height above sea level.

Summit temperature estimates

On mountain pages, we show a rough temperature at the top, worked out from the nearest city in our weather data and the average cooling of the air with height. It is a textbook estimate for a calm day, so use it only to get a feel for how cold a high peak is. The real value depends on wind, cloud, season and local weather, and it can be far colder.

Where to go next

The country pages group features by country. The guides explain how each kind of figure is measured. If you are planning a trip, our other sections give the weather of cities, the plug types of countries and the visa facilities for Indian passport holders; they are on the same trips site.

Why is my favourite mountain not listed?

It may not meet our rules for inclusion: a recorded height, a country on Earth and links to many Wikipedia editions.

Can I trust the figures for climbing or navigation?

No. Use official surveys and current guidebooks for anything where safety is involved.

About these figures. Heights, areas and lengths come from Wikidata (CC0), downloaded on 14 November 2026. Measurements differ between sources, and rivers and lakes change; where Wikidata holds several values we use the middle one and say so. Treat the numbers as good estimates, not survey results.
