How river lengths and lake areas are measured

The longest river and the biggest lake are questions with several honest answers.

The river problem

Measuring a river sounds easy: follow it from the source to the mouth. The trouble starts at both ends. Where does a river begin? A river is fed by many streams, and each has its own source. The convention is to follow the longest stream upstream from the mouth, or the one with the greatest flow, but these may not be the same. Where does the river end? At the sea, but many rivers broaden into a wide estuary or split into a delta with many channels, and any line through them is a choice.

Then there is the question of how finely to measure. A river that twists in large loops is longer than the straight line, and a measurement made on a coarse map gives a shorter answer than one made on a detailed one, because small bends are ignored. This is sometimes called the coastline paradox: the finer the ruler, the longer the length.

Why the top is disputed

Two rivers have long been argued over as the longest in the world, and the figures recorded for each can differ by hundreds of kilometres depending on which source and which mouth are used. You can see this in the data on this site: the highest and lowest recorded lengths for the longest rivers are far apart. Our rule is to show the middle value and say when sources differ. Where a river has several recorded lengths, we ignore any below 75% of the largest, since those usually measure only a section, and use the middle of the rest.

Rivers change too

A river changes course, builds deltas and, in some places, is dammed or diverted. Meanders are cut off and become oxbow lakes. The length of a river in a year of drought can differ from its length in a flood, and a river that reaches the sea in the wet season may dry up before it does in the dry season.

The lake problem

A lake's area depends on the water level, which depends on the season, on rainfall and on how much water people take out. Some lakes shrink and grow by a large fraction of their area. Many shallow lakes in dry regions come and go with the rains. Large reservoirs behind dams change with the use of the water. The area of a lake is therefore a figure for a particular time, usually an average or a typical value, and sources choose different times.

There is also the question of what counts as one lake. Lakes Michigan and Huron are joined by a wide channel and are the same body of water to a hydrologist, but they are two lakes in common usage. A ranking that counts them separately has different numbers from one that counts them together. We follow the common usage and leave out composite entries that Wikidata records as a single item.

Salt lakes and seas

Some of the world's largest bodies of water are salt lakes, and one of them is called a sea. Whether a salt water body counts as a lake or a sea is a matter of definition. The largest of them is listed in our ranking of lakes because geographers class it as the world's largest inland body of water.

Depth and volume

The depth of a lake is the greatest depth recorded, which is the single deepest spot, not the average. Volume, which many people would find a better measure of size, depends on the shape of the whole basin and is known accurately for few lakes. We show depth where Wikidata has it and do not rank by volume.

How to read our rankings

Treat each rank as "about here". Where two rivers or lakes are within a few percent of each other, their order is uncertain. Where the gaps are large, the order is safe. The pages show the neighbours of each feature so you can see how close the figures are.

Is the Nile or the Amazon longer?

It depends on the source and mouth you choose. Our data record several lengths for both; see their pages.

Why is a lake's area different on another website?

It may be quoting another year, a high or low water level, or a different definition of the shoreline.

About these figures. Heights, areas and lengths come from Wikidata (CC0), downloaded on 14 November 2026. Measurements differ between sources, and rivers and lakes change; where Wikidata holds several values we use the middle one and say so. Treat the numbers as good estimates, not survey results.
