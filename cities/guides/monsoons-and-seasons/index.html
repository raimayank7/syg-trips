Monsoons and seasons explained

Many of the cities on this site have a dry season and a wet season rather than summer and winter. Here is what that means for a trip.

What a monsoon is

The word monsoon comes from an Arabic word for season. It describes a wind that changes direction with the seasons. In summer the land heats up faster than the sea, warm air rises over the land and moist air is pulled in from the ocean, bringing heavy rain. In winter the land cools, the wind turns around, and the air is dry. This is why a place under a monsoon can see almost no rain for six months and then a great deal in the following four.

South Asia has the best-known monsoon. For most of India the rain comes with the southwest monsoon, in the months from about June to September, and the rest of the year is far drier. Some parts have a second wet season: the southeastern coast of the peninsula receives much of its rain later in the year from a different wind. Other parts of the world have monsoon patterns too, including parts of Southeast Asia, West Africa, northern Australia and the south west of North America. Each has its own dates.

How to spot it on a climate table

Look at the Rain column on any city page. A monsoon city shows a clear hump: several months with very little rain, a rise, and then a few months with several times more. Our text calls this concentrated rain, for example "June to September bring about 80% of the year's rain". Compare that with a city whose rain is spread through the year, where no month is more than about twice another.

Humidity follows the rain. In a monsoon the humidity rises sharply with the first rains and falls again afterwards. The hottest month is often just before the rains, because the sky is clear and the air is dry; this is why the hottest daytime temperatures in India usually come in the weeks before the monsoon arrives, not in the rainy season itself.

Planning around the rains

A wet season does not mean constant rain. In many monsoon cities the rain falls in a few heavy bursts a day, often at the same time each day, with sun between them. It can be the best time for green landscapes, waterfalls, lower prices and fewer visitors. It can also be the worst time for travel: flooded roads, landslides in hills, cancelled boats and trains, and mosquitoes and waterborne illness.

If your dates fall in a wet season:

  1. Leave slack in your schedule so that a cancelled transport link does not wreck the whole trip.
  2. Choose places that are better in the rain, such as city museums and forts, rather than ones that need clear views.
  3. Check the official travel advisories and local forecasts close to your dates, because the start and strength of the monsoon vary from year to year.
  4. Pack for it: see the packing line on each city page, and the packing lists.

Other season patterns

Not every city follows a monsoon. Many mid-latitude cities have four seasons, with the rain either spread evenly or concentrated in winter, as in the Mediterranean countries. Cities close to the equator can have two rainy seasons each year with little temperature change. Desert cities may have almost no rain at all, and then a heavy storm in one month of the year. The chart on each city page shows which pattern a city has: look at the shape of the rain bars before you read the numbers.

When does the monsoon start?

It varies by place and by year. Use the climate table to see which months are wet in your city, and look for the weather service's announcement close to your dates; it changes every year.

Is it safe to travel in the rains?

It depends on where you go and on your plans. Hills and rivers can be risky in heavy rain. Check the official travel and weather advice of the country you are visiting rather than relying on this site.

Check before you travel. Climate averages describe a typical year from long records (NASA POWER data, refreshed November 2026). They are not a forecast, and they do not cover visas, prices, safety or closures. Check the official sources for those and the weather forecast for your dates.
