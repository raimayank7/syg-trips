How to read climate data for a trip

A climate table is a long-term average, not a forecast. Read it the right way and it is the best free planning tool for a trip.

Weather and climate are different things

Weather is what happens on a particular day: the rain this afternoon or the heat wave next week. Climate is the pattern over many years. When this site says that a city has an average high of 31 degrees C in May, it means that if you collected the highest temperature of every May day over a long run of years, the typical value would be 31. Some days will be several degrees hotter or colder. For a trip planned months ahead, the climate is what you can know. A forecast only becomes useful in the last week or so.

The columns, one by one

High is the typical afternoon temperature. On this site it is estimated as the month's mean temperature plus half the usual gap between a day's warmest and coolest moment, and it tells you how hot a day of sightseeing will feel. Low is the matching estimate for the coolest moment, usually just before sunrise. It tells you whether you need a jacket in the evening or whether the night will be too warm to sleep without a fan. Mean is the average over the whole day and night, which is why it sits between the two.

Rain is the total rainfall for the month in millimetres. As a guide, under 30 mm is a dry month, 30 to 100 mm is a month with some rain, and above 200 mm is very wet. The figure does not say how many days it rains. A month with 300 mm may have a few huge downpours or steady drizzle on most days, so the monthly total is best read together with the local knowledge in a good guidebook.

Humidity is the average relative humidity. At 30 degrees C, 40% humidity feels pleasant and 85% feels heavy and sticky. High humidity makes heat harder to bear and cold feel colder.

Sunshine here is the solar energy reaching the ground in kilowatt-hours per square metre per day. A cloudless day in the tropics gives around 6 to 7. A grey winter day in a northern city gives well under 1. Compare months of the same city, or cities at similar latitudes, rather than a polar city against a desert.

What the averages hide

Averages smooth out the extremes. A city with an average high of 28 degrees C in October can still have a week of 35. Mountain cities have sharp changes with height: the data are for a grid of about 50 kilometres, so a hill station close to a plain may read warmer than the town itself. Coastal and island cities can feel cooler than the grid because of the sea breeze, or wetter because of local showers. If you are going to a small town in the hills, use the nearest big city as a rough guide and expect it to be cooler and wetter.

Years also differ. Monsoons arrive late or early, a warm year follows a cool one, and long-term warming is shifting averages slowly. The numbers on this site describe a typical recent year, not next month.

Three quick ways to use the table

  1. Look for the extremes first. Find the hottest and wettest months. If both are the same month, as in many tropical cities, that month is the one to avoid or to prepare for.
  2. Check the gap between high and low. A gap of 15 degrees or more means hot days and cool nights, so pack layers. A gap under 8 means the day and night feel similar.
  3. Compare two cities. If you are choosing between two places, use the compare tool and read the rain column first; it separates the cities more than temperature does.
Are these numbers from weather stations?

No. They are climatology from NASA's POWER project, which combines satellite observations and computer models into a regular grid, so every city is measured the same way. That is good for comparing places and slightly less exact than a local station for a particular spot.

Why do you show humidity and sunshine?

Heat and rain alone do not describe how a day feels. A humid 30 degrees C and a dry 30 degrees C are very different, and a month with plenty of sun and little rain is better for photographs and outdoor plans than one with the same temperature under cloud.

Can I use this to plan a trip a year ahead?

Yes, for choosing the season. Not for choosing a day. Pick the month from the climate, then look at the forecast when your dates come close.

Check before you travel. Climate averages describe a typical year from long records (NASA POWER data, refreshed November 2026). They are not a forecast, and they do not cover visas, prices, safety or closures. Check the official sources for those and the weather forecast for your dates.
