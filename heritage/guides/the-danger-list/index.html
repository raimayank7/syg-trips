The List of World Heritage in Danger

The danger list is a warning flag and a call for help. It is not a ban, and it is not a punishment.

What it is

UNESCO keeps a second list inside the World Heritage List: the List of World Heritage in Danger. A site goes on it when it faces serious and specific threats, so that the world pays attention and the country can get help. You can see the sites that are marked as in danger in our data on the in-danger page.

Why a site is put on it

Threats fall into two broad groups. Some come from events: armed conflict, earthquake, flood, fire. Others build up over time: unplanned development, mining, large infrastructure, looting, pollution, the effects of climate change, neglect or lack of money for upkeep. The World Heritage Committee decides when a site is in danger, usually after a report from experts, and it often asks the country for a plan to put things right.

What happens next

When a site is placed on the list, UNESCO and the country agree on a set of "corrective measures" and a target for taking the site off the list again. The site can get extra technical help and sometimes funding. The Committee reviews the site every year. If the threats are removed, the site comes off the danger list. If they are not, it can stay for many years. In rare cases, a site that loses the qualities that made it special can be deleted from the World Heritage List altogether.

What it means for visitors

The danger list says something about the site, not about whether you can visit. Some sites on the list are open and lightly affected. Some are partly closed. Some are in places where conflict makes travel unsafe, and some cannot be visited at all. Check three things:

  1. The site's own official page, for what is open and what is closed.
  2. Your government's travel advice for the country, which tells you about safety, not heritage. India's Ministry of External Affairs and other governments publish advisories.
  3. Recent news from reliable sources for the area.

If you do visit, follow the rules closely. A site that is already under pressure suffers more from careless visitors.

Can visitors help?

Yes, a little. Visit responsibly, pay the entry fee, hire local guides and buy local products where the site is open, because tourism income can pay for upkeep. Do not visit places where it is unsafe or where visitors are not welcome. If you want to help from home, many sites have official friends or support groups; check that a charity is registered before you give.

Reading our in-danger page

The in-danger page lists every site marked in the UNESCO data we downloaded on 14 November 2026. The list changes when the Committee meets, so a site may have been added or removed since. Always check UNESCO's current list before relying on it.

Does being on the danger list mean the site will lose its status?

No. The list is meant to help a site recover. Very few sites have ever been removed from the World Heritage List.

Can I still visit a site on the danger list?

Often yes, but not always. Check the site's own page and your government's travel advice first.
