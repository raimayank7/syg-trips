How to visit World Heritage sites responsibly

Many of these places have survived centuries and can be harmed in a season. A few habits make your visit lighter.

Plan with the site, not against it

Check the official page of the site before you travel. It shows opening times, ticket rules and any closures or limits on numbers. Many sites close for a day each week or at certain times of year, and some require tickets booked in advance. Do not rely on a blog written years ago.

Go at the quiet times if you can. Early morning and the shoulder seasons mean fewer crowds, a better experience and less wear on the site. Our city pages show which months are most comfortable; the comfortable months are often also the most crowded, so consider the month on either side.

On the ground

Follow the signs. Rules at a heritage site are there because somebody found a reason: a wall that was damaged by hands, a floor worn by feet, a bird that left after being disturbed. Do not climb on structures, touch paintings or carvings, or step off marked paths. Do not take anything away, not even a small stone or shell.

Keep your voice down at places of worship and at quiet natural sites. Dress in a way that suits the place. At religious sites, cover shoulders and knees where that is expected, and remove shoes where asked.

Photography

Many sites allow photographs, with some limits. Flash can harm old paintings and fabrics, drones are banned or need permission at many sites, and tripods may be restricted. Look for signs, or ask at the ticket desk. Ask before photographing people, and respect a refusal.

Guides and local people

A good local guide can show you much more than you would see alone, and pays local people a fair wage. Agree the price first and use guides who are licensed where licensing exists. Buy food, crafts and drinks locally. Many World Heritage sites are living places with residents who work and go to school there: treat the streets as someone's neighbourhood, not a stage.

Waste, water and wildlife

Carry your rubbish out if there are no bins. Take a refillable bottle and avoid single-use plastic. In natural sites, keep to trails, keep your distance from animals, do not feed them, and do not pick plants. In protected marine areas, do not touch coral or stand on it, and use sunscreen that the local rules allow.

Safety

Some sites are remote or in places with limited help, and some are in countries where your government advises care. Read your government's travel advice for the destination before you book, take out travel insurance that suits the activities you plan, and tell someone your plans when you head into remote areas. For visa rules, check the destination country's official site; our visa section lists what the Indian Ministry of External Affairs publishes.

Be a good guest

Buy a ticket even when you could slip in. Ticket income often pays for the upkeep of the site. Leave a review that is honest and helpful, not one that sends more people to the most fragile spot. And remember that you are visiting a place that other people want to see in a hundred years.

Are drones allowed at World Heritage sites?

Often not, or only with a permit. Check the site's rules and the country's drone law before you pack one.

Is it all right to touch ancient stones?

Usually not. Oils from hands wear stone and paint away over time, even if the damage is not visible after one touch.
