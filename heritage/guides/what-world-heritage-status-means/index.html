What World Heritage status means

A World Heritage label is a recognition, not a ticket office or a guarantee. Here is what it is and what it is not.

The idea

Some places matter to people everywhere, not only to the country they are in. A great temple, a fossil site, a rainforest, an old city: if they are lost, something is lost for everyone. In 1972, the member countries of UNESCO adopted a convention to protect such places, and the World Heritage List grew out of it. A country that has signed the convention can propose places on its own territory. The first sites were added in 1978, and the list now has well over a thousand.

How a place gets on the list

The process is slow on purpose. A country first puts a place on a "tentative list". Later it prepares a detailed nomination that describes the site, shows that it meets at least one of ten criteria (see the ten criteria), and sets out how it will be protected and managed. Independent experts then assess the nomination, and the World Heritage Committee, made up of representatives of member countries, decides at its yearly meeting. It may accept the site, ask for more information, or refuse.

Cultural, natural and mixed

Every site is one of three categories. Cultural sites are valued for what people built or created: monuments, cities, landscapes shaped by farming, places of belief. Natural sites are valued for the natural world: mountains, reefs, forests, geological formations, habitats of rare species. Mixed sites qualify on both counts. The cultural sites make up the large majority of the list.

What status gives a site

The label can help a site in several ways. It draws attention and visitors. It can bring technical help and sometimes money for conservation. It commits the country to protect the site, and it invites outside scrutiny if the site is threatened. When a site is in serious trouble, it can be placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger (see the danger list), and in rare cases it can be removed from the list altogether.

What status does not give

It does not make a place safe, open or cheap to visit. It does not set ticket prices or opening hours, which belong to the site's manager. It does not guarantee that the site is well kept, although the system exists to push in that direction. And it does not mean that every part of a famous place is included: a listing may cover only a core area and a buffer zone, or several separate parts, which are sometimes far apart.

What it means for you as a visitor

Expect more visitors than at an unlisted place, and plan for the crowds: go early or in the shoulder season. Expect rules that protect the site: limits on photography, no climbing, restricted areas, and a ticket for entry. Expect a better chance of finding good information on site. And expect that some sites are remote, expensive or difficult to reach, because nature and ancient history do not place themselves near airports.

Is every World Heritage site open to visitors?

No. Some are in conflict zones, some are fragile and restricted, and some are inhabited towns or working landscapes where you walk around freely. Check the site's own page and your government's travel advice.

Does a site lose its status if it is damaged?

Not at once. UNESCO first works with the country, may add the site to the danger list, and only in extreme cases removes it from the list.
