The ten criteria explained

Every World Heritage site meets at least one of ten criteria. The numbers on a site page are short codes for them.

Why there are criteria

A place does not get on the World Heritage List because it is old, famous or beautiful. It must be of "outstanding universal value", which UNESCO tests against ten criteria. The first six are for cultural sites and the last four for natural sites. A site may meet one criterion or several. Mixed sites meet at least one of each kind.

The following are our own plain-words summaries. They are not UNESCO's official wording, which you can find on UNESCO's website.

The six cultural criteria

  • (i) The site is a masterpiece of human creative genius: a work so remarkable that it stands alone, such as a unique building or a unique work of art or planning.
  • (ii) It shows an important exchange of human values, ideas or skills over a period of time or across a region, in architecture, technology, art, town planning or landscape.
  • (iii) It is an exceptional testimony to a cultural tradition or a civilisation that is living or has vanished, such as an ancient city or a way of life.
  • (iv) It is an outstanding example of a type of building, architectural or technological ensemble or landscape that illustrates a stage in human history.
  • (v) It is an outstanding example of a traditional way of settling or using land or sea, particularly when it is threatened by change: terraced farms, villages, island communities.
  • (vi) It is directly linked with events, living traditions, ideas, beliefs or works of outstanding significance. This criterion is used with care and normally together with another.

The four natural criteria

  • (vii) The site has superlative natural phenomena or areas of exceptional natural beauty and importance: great waterfalls, canyons, mountain ranges.
  • (viii) It is an outstanding example of the major stages of the Earth's history, including the record of life and of the processes shaping landforms: fossil beds, glaciers, volcanoes.
  • (ix) It is an outstanding example of ongoing ecological and biological processes in the evolution of land, water and sea ecosystems and communities of plants and animals.
  • (x) It holds the most important and significant natural habitats for conserving biological diversity, including those with threatened species of outstanding value.

How to read a site page

On each site page here, the criteria are shown as letters in brackets and spelt out in a sentence. A cultural site with criteria (ii), (iii) and (iv), for example, is valued for how it shows the exchange of ideas, how it bears witness to a tradition, and for being an outstanding example of its type. A natural site with (vii) and (x) combines scenery with habitat.

You can use the criteria to decide what to look for when you visit. A (iv) site is about architecture or an ensemble: look at how the parts fit together. A (viii) site is about the Earth's story: look for the rocks and landforms. A (v) site is about people and land: look at how they live and farm.

Limits

The criteria numbers give a short summary and nothing more. UNESCO's own statement for each site explains in detail why it was accepted. If you are curious, read it on UNESCO's website: it is usually a good short essay on the place.

Why do many sites have several criteria?

Because most places are important in more than one way. A cathedral may be a masterpiece, a good example of its type, and a witness to a tradition.

Which criterion is the most common?

You can see this for yourself on the site pages. Cultural criteria are used far more than natural ones because cultural sites far outnumber natural sites on the list.
