How to pace the days of a trip

Most itineraries fail because they are too full. A few rules of pace make them work.

The common mistake

When we plan a trip we remember how much there is to see and forget how long it takes to see it. A plan that lists six sights in a day looks rich on paper and feels like a race in reality: every entry has a queue, every journey has a delay, and every meal takes longer than you thought. The result is a trip you remember as tiring. The fix is simple: plan less.

A rule of thumb

For most travellers, one main thing in the morning, one in the afternoon and one easy thing in the evening is a full day. A main thing is something that takes a few hours: a fort, a museum, a hike, a temple complex. Everything else, such as a market, a viewpoint or a walk, is an extra that you do if there is time. On a day with a lot of travel, plan only one main thing, or none.

Treat travel days as days

A journey of several hours is an activity, not a gap. Count the time to get to the station or airport, waiting, the journey, and getting from the arrival point to your bed. After a long journey you will be tired, so the day you arrive should be light: check in, a short walk, an early dinner. The day you leave should be light for the same reason, and should leave a buffer before the departure time, because traffic and queues take longer than the map says.

Put meals and rests into the plan

Meals are part of the day. Write them down, with the time, so that you do not find yourself hungry in the wrong place. Plan a rest in the middle of the day when it is hot or when children or elders are with you. A rest is not wasted time; it is what lets you enjoy the evening.

Group things by place

Look at a map and group the activities that are close together on the same day, so that you do not cross the town three times. When you cannot avoid a long journey, choose the activities at the far end of it for that day and stay there longer.

Keep the fixed and the flexible apart

Some items are fixed: a ticketed entry at 10:00, a train at 15:30. Some are flexible: a walk, a market, a meal. In the planner, give the fixed ones a time and leave the flexible ones without. Then the day has a skeleton, and the rest can move when something takes longer.

Build in slack

Leave a gap of an hour or two in every day, or a whole free half-day in a longer trip. Use it for what goes wrong, such as a delay or a closed attraction, and for what goes right, such as a place you want to stay in longer. A trip with no slack has no room for the best surprises.

Be honest about energy

People with small children, elders or health conditions need more rest, more bathroom stops and less walking. Plan for the slowest person, not the fastest. People who are on their feet all day need an easy morning after. A trip that is too hard for one person is a bad trip for everyone.

Check your plan with these questions

  1. Is there more than one main thing in the morning or afternoon?
  2. Is there a long journey on the same day as a big visit?
  3. Is there time for meals and a rest?
  4. Is there a buffer before any ticketed event or departure?
  5. Is the last day light?

If you answer yes to the first two or no to the last three for any day, simplify that day.

How many days should I allow for a place?

That depends on the place and you. Read about the place, count its main things, and allow about a half-day for each main thing plus time to arrive and leave.

Is it worth planning in the evening?

A relaxed dinner and a walk make a good evening. A full programme after a full day wears people out.
