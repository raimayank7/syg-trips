When the lists disagree: what to do

You may find one source saying visa free and another saying e-visa for the same country. Here is how to decide.

Why sources differ

Visa rules are decided by each country's government, and they change. Governments sometimes announce a change a few days before it starts, and sometimes change the rule for a limited period. A list kept in India by the Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) can lag behind a decision made in another capital. Different pages on the MEA site can also have been updated on different dates: the page this site is based on carried a recent update date, while older separate pages for the same facilities were last updated years earlier. Travel blogs, airline pages and agents add another layer, and they may be copying one another or older information.

The result is that you will sometimes see two sources saying different things about the same country. This does not always mean that one is wrong: a country may have more than one rule at once, for example visa free for people with a visa from certain other countries, and an e-visa for everyone else.

The order of trust

  1. The destination country's own immigration or foreign ministry website. It makes the rule, so it is the final word.
  2. The country's embassy or high commission in India. It answers for its government, and you can write to it or call it.
  3. The MEA's page for Indian citizens. It is an official Indian source and is useful to start with, but it is a copy of other governments' decisions, and it can lag.
  4. Your airline. Airlines check your documents before you board, and they follow the industry's own database. They can tell you what they will accept, but their answer is not an official ruling.
  5. Travel blogs, forums and agents. Use them for ideas, not for the final answer.

What to do when you find a conflict

Write down what each source says, with the date. Go to the first source in the order above that you can reach. If the destination's site is unclear, write to the embassy and keep the reply. If the rule is conditional, check which condition applies to you: your passport, your other visas, the purpose and length of your trip.

If two official sources still disagree, take the stricter one. A stricter plan, for example applying for an e-visa when visa free might be allowed, costs a little time and money. A looser plan that turns out to be wrong can cost the whole trip.

Re-check before you fly

Check again a few days before you travel. A rule you read when you booked, months earlier, may have changed. Keep a screenshot of the page you relied on, with its date, in case anyone asks.

This site's approach

Each country page shows the MEA list's wording, the date the MEA page was last updated and the date we downloaded it, so that you can see how old the information is. When we find that two MEA pages disagree about a country, we say so. We do not try to guess which one is right; we point you to the destination's own site.

Which should I trust if the MEA says visa free but the embassy says e-visa?

The embassy. It speaks for the country that makes the rule.

Can an airline refuse me even if the list says visa free?

Yes, if its check finds a condition you do not meet, such as passport validity or a missing return ticket. Ask the airline early.
